Now, here's a detail that always surprises our visitors. The most common fossil of the Ancients isn't a human bone. It's a chicken bone. In twenty eighteen, British researchers showed that the chicken raised by the Ancients was a new animal: at least twice the size of a medieval chicken, with a skeleton, bone chemistry and genetics unlike any of its ancestors. And there were about twenty-three billion of them alive at the same time. To a paleontologist of the future, this may very well be the age of the chicken.

But there's one mark no future scientist will miss. Starting in the nineteen fifties, atomic bomb tests spread plutonium across the entire planet. It fell with the rain and settled at the bottom of lakes and seas, in a thin layer, all over the world. Plutonium two thirty-nine takes about twenty-four thousand years to lose half its radioactivity. Ten thousand years later, the layer is still there, easy to detect. At a lake in Canada, Crawford Lake, the Ancients' geologists even proposed this layer as the start of a new epoch of the Earth: the Anthropocene. In twenty twenty-four, the proposal was rejected. The Ancients couldn't even agree on that.

And there's an even subtler mark: the air. In Antarctica, every layer of snow that turns into ice traps tiny bubbles of the air of its time. Before factories, the air held about two hundred eighty parts per million of carbon dioxide. In May twenty twenty-five, the peak went past four hundred thirty. Whoever drills into the ice from this era will find, trapped in bubbles, the breath of a civilization in a hurry.

And there's one message the Ancients wrote to the future on purpose. They buried their most dangerous nuclear waste in tunnels hundreds of meters deep. In Finland, the first repository of its kind, called Onkalo, received a license to operate in October twenty twenty-six. But how do you warn someone, thousands of years from now, not to dig there? No language lasts that long. In the United States, a report from nineteen ninety-three drafted the text of just such a warning. It begins like this: This place is not a place of honor. Nothing valued is here. It's strange and beautiful at the same time: one of the Ancients' first letters to the future was a request to stay away.

Now let's go further. One million years. The cities have become a layer of compressed rubble. The plastic has become a dark sheet between the rocks. In twenty eighteen, two scientists, Gavin Schmidt, of NASA, and Adam Frank, asked an uncomfortable question: if an industrial civilization had existed on Earth millions of years ago, would we be able to tell? Their answer: it wouldn't be easy. Given enough time, an entire civilization can become just a strange band in the rock: a shift in carbon, traces of plastic, rare elements, that from far away looks like other natural changes in Earth's past. Eight billion people. A century in a hurry. And in the end, a thin band of different rock.

The clearest traces of the Ancients will be far from here. On the Moon, where there's no wind or rain, the astronauts' footprints could last millions of years. About twenty-two thousand miles up, dead satellites, pushed into an orbit called the graveyard, could keep circling for millions of years. Two probes, the Voyagers, carry a golden record with sounds and images of Earth, made to last about a billion years among the stars. And the Ancients' radio broadcasts already form a bubble around the Earth with a radius of more than a hundred light-years.

But there's one thing the Ancients were slow to realize. The layer they would leave behind hadn't been written yet. Every layer of rock is just a record of choices. More plastic or less. More carbon or less. More bombs or less. And they started to choose. When they discovered that a gas from refrigerators and spray cans was opening a hole in the ozone layer, they signed an agreement, the Montreal Protocol, in nineteen eighty-seven. And the layer began to recover. They recorded archives built to last for centuries. They buried the most dangerous waste deep underground, with warnings for whoever came after. And they started measuring what they were leaving in the ground, precisely so they could change it.

The Ancients were brilliant and completely lost at the same time. But they were the first civilization that knew it was turning into rock. And that gave them a chance no people before them ever had: deciding what would be left.
